The Finance Ministry shifts budget protection from oil to bank deposits

The State Duma has passed a three-year budget where commodity vulnerability is offset by progressive personal income tax and investors' money

25 November 2026

The State Duma has in its final third reading passed the draft federal budget for 2027–2029, establishing a new operating principle for public finance. The authorities no longer view external market fluctuations as the main threat to the treasury. Commodity vulnerability is being softened by restructuring domestic revenue sources, with a primary focus on redistributing private savings.

The main instrument of this reform will be the unification of tax regimes and a review of existing optimization mechanisms. From 2027, Russia plans to combine salaries, deposit income and investment returns into a single progressive personal income tax scale ranging from 13% to 22%. The changes will directly affect traditional capital-structuring vehicles: the government is raising taxes on personal funds and closed-end mutual funds.

The Finance Ministry's logic is clear. Amid volatile export revenues, domestic savings offer the most reliable source of funds. The ministry is shifting its centre of gravity from commodity rent to absorbing liquidity from the public and investors, creating a level playing field for all forms of personal income. Rather than waiting for high commodity prices, the treasury is moving towards aggressive domestic capital extraction.

This pivot in fiscal policy changes conditions for every asset owner in Russia. This analysis addresses two central questions: what mechanism has allowed the authorities to stop fearing a drop in oil prices, and whose savings will fund the deficit reduction promised by the Finance Ministry.

Official claims regarding the budget's resilience to external shocks clash with the arithmetic of the fiscal deficit. The budget law for the current year projected a deficit of 3.786 trn roubles, or 1.6% of GDP. By early autumn, the Finance Ministry had drastically revised these estimates, nearly doubling the projected shortfall to 7.345 trn roubles, or 3.2% of GDP. In November, Anton Siluanov, the finance minister, attempted to reassure the market by promising to keep the final figure around 3% of GDP or slightly lower. Yet the scale of missing revenue remains near record levels.

Officials' confidence in the system's stability stems from the fact that falling export revenues are being offset by domestic resources. The Finance Ministry estimates that non-oil and gas revenues will exceed the original target by more than 1 trn roubles. The commodity sector, meanwhile, is losing ground. Despite favourable market conditions, oil and gas export revenues will fall short of targets. Monthly budget dynamics highlight this instability: after surpluses of 600bn roubles in August and 609bn roubles in September, execution slipped back into a deficit of 48bn roubles in October.

It is precisely the volatile nature of revenue inflows that is forcing the financial authorities to fundamentally overhaul how the budget is funded. The declared reduction in dependence on oil prices means only one thing in practice: commodity market volatility is being transferred from foreign buyers to domestic taxpayers. Officials no longer count on windfall hydrocarbon rents. Consequently, the modest downward adjustment of the expected deficit from 3.2% to 3% of GDP is not being achieved through spending cuts. Instead, the Finance Ministry is laying the groundwork to siphon the missing trillions directly from the incomes of citizens and businesses.

The mechanism designed to insulate the budget from external shocks relies on artificially capping the spending of oil revenues. From next year, the government is lowering the cut-off price in its fiscal rule, obliging the authorities to funnel excess commodity revenue into reserves at the earliest opportunity. Even though total oil and gas receipts for the year will fall short of targets, the Finance Ministry expects to add roughly 1 trn roubles to the National Wealth Fund. Withdrawing this money from circulation means the treasury requires alternative fuel to cover current spending.

Domestic savings provide that alternative fuel. The planned merger from 2027 of salaries, deposit interest and investment income into a single progressive income tax scale of 13% to 22% eliminates the previous gap between labor and capital income. Liquidity leaks are also being plugged at the corporate level: higher tax burdens on closed-end mutual funds and personal funds strip wealthy investors of familiar optimization vehicles. The state will take a maximum slice of any asset appreciation, granting no exceptions for tailored financial structures.

The second element of this mechanism is the forced extension of private capital's time horizon by steering it into state-managed instruments. A project designed to channel savings into the Long-Term Savings Programme offers citizens state co-financing of up to 36,000 roubles a year alongside tax deductions, in exchange for surrendering immediate control over their capital. The budget secures a source of funding locked in for years ahead, replacing lost foreign borrowing. The private sector is not merely sharing profits via a progressive tax scale, but directly financing state priorities through illiquid vehicles.

Regional budget performance illustrates the fragility of the state system's apparent health. According to Expert RA, regional budgets recorded a combined surplus of 28.5bn roubles in the first nine months of 2026. Compared with a deficit of 149.3bn roubles over the same period last year, the figures suggest a turnaround. Yet a broader comparison reveals the true scale of the decline: the current result is nearly 30 times lower than in 2024, when the combined regional surplus reached 849.1bn roubles.

The illusion of regional strength is created by windfall revenues in just a few territories, while resource-producing hinterlands slip into the red. The overall surplus relies on Moscow with its 241bn rouble surplus, Tatarstan with 86.5bn roubles and Krasnoyarsk Krai with 31.3bn roubles. At the other extreme are the country's main oil-producing region, Khanty-Mansi Autonomous Okrug (KhMAO), with a deficit of 37.2bn roubles, Irkutsk Region with a shortfall of 39.4bn roubles and Nizhny Novgorod Region with a gap of 31.1bn roubles. Falling revenues in oil-and-gas-producing regions reflect the same vulnerability that the Finance Ministry is attempting to smooth over at the federal level.

This regional divide underscores a broader rule of Russia's modern fiscal architecture. The economic model can no longer rely on raw-material sales: export rents are shrinking, leaving extraction regions to face rising spending alone. Personal income tax and consumer spending, concentrated in major urban hubs, remain the only reliable sources of local revenue. The state system is shifting from extracting natural resources to absorbing domestic liquidity, turning every income tax payer into a fundamental pillar of the budget.

State financial policy is not, of course, confined entirely to relentless tax increases. The system retains the capacity for targeted relief in vulnerable sectors where a sudden drain of liquidity could disrupt goods supply chains. In the approved budget package, the State Duma modified the SPOT system, allowing importers to replace advance VAT and excise collateral payments with bank guarantees, deferring tax payments to the 20th of the following month. This operational concession temporarily eases working capital for trade intermediaries, but does not alter the overall direction of fiscal policy.

Such targeted measures are intended to smooth structural disruptions in foreign trade while the rest of the tax-extraction architecture adjusts to new progressive rates. The regulator is willing to reduce the administrative and financial burden on import supply chains to maintain supply and prevent price spikes. Yet any relief at the point of import is clawed back during profit distribution. Capital freed up by bank guarantees is inevitably extracted later through progressive personal income tax on dividends, taxes on bank deposit interest and higher levies on investment funds.

The three-year budget law for 2027–2029, formally approved by the State Duma, draws a line under a former era of state planning. The trajectory of the country's financial system is no longer dictated by crude oil prices or the volume of hydrocarbon rents. The authorities have finalized a model in which any shortfall in export revenues is offset by extracting capital domestically. The enacted budget enshrines this reality: the Finance Ministry no longer needs to protect investors from external economic shocks, as long as investors' money protects the budget itself.
