Russian diesel deal hits burnt-out refineries

As tankers manoeuvre in the Atlantic, oil refining has fallen by a third and the budget prepares to audit miners' tax breaks.

10 November 2026

The Malta-flagged tanker Agioi Fanendes left the Baltic port of Primorsk on 22 October 2026, carrying 40,000 tonnes (around 300,000 barrels) of ultra-low-sulphur diesel. It was carrying the first commercial batch under a publicly announced deal to supply Russian fuel to curb prices at American petrol stations. The schedule called for arrival at US East Coast terminals by 7 November.

As it approached North America, its Greek operator, Eastmed, changed the declared destination to "for orders". Roughly 400 miles off the New York coast, the vessel slowed down and began manoeuvring in international waters. The Agioi Fanendes was the third tanker carrying Russian diesel in a month forced to turn back or conceal its final destination.

The idling tankers in the Atlantic highlighted a basic anomaly: a political "energy truce" hit the physical destruction of refineries. Ambitious export plans quickly lose meaning when key refineries are knocked out and shipping lines lose faith in the stability of port shipments.

This analysis charts the crisis. First, it shows how renewed drone attacks on domestic refineries cut primary refining capacity and drained export contracts of physical supply. Second, it explains why shipowners are abandoning Russian routes for more profitable freight elsewhere. Finally, it assesses how the Accounts Chamber, faced with falling oil and gas revenues, is moving to audit miners' tax breaks and initiating tough arbitration proceedings.

The formal reason for turning back the tankers was legal risk in America. The governor of New Jersey, Mikie Sherrill, warned port authorities and terminal operators of civil and criminal penalties for receiving Russian products. Donald Trump's attempt to lower petrol prices by bypassing his own sanctions legislation ran into state-level resistance. Yet legal bans merely accelerated the collapse of agreements that were already losing physical footing.

The true rupture occurred at Russian refineries, not American ports. A resumption of drone attacks after a brief pause disrupted nine of the country's 35 major refineries in a single week. Strikes hit key plants in the Ryazan, Nizhny Novgorod, Samara, Saratov and Leningrad regions, as well as Bashkortostan and Krasnodar Krai. A strike on the Kirishi refinery, the country's second largest, caused a fire and shut down processing lines.

Total capacity at hit refineries reached 300,000 tonnes a day (2.2m barrels) since late October, almost a third of Russia's total primary refining capacity of 930,000 tonnes. Actual daily crude runs fell from 700,000 to 500,000 tonnes. A nearly 30% drop in refining stripped export contracts of physical diesel while the tankers were en route.

For Russia's oil industry, losing up to 200,000 tonnes of daily refining created a double dead end. The deal with Washington envisaged shipping 300,000 tonnes of diesel initially, eventually rising to 1m tonnes. Amid worsening domestic fuel shortages, continuing seaborne exports became physically impossible. The attempt to play the American market crossed the ocean only to run into burnt-out units.

The damage to refining immediately spilled over into maritime logistics. The deal's scale—from an initial 300,000 tonnes to regular shipments of 1m tonnes—required smooth execution and full fleet utilisation from tanker operators. But repeated drone strikes knocked out 300,000 tonnes of daily capacity out of total primary capacity of 930,000 tonnes. The supply chain ran dry. The tanker fleet faced long delays at Baltic terminals with no guaranteed fuel volumes.

A second hit came from the departure of key European carriers. In October Greek shipowners more than halved shipments of Russian crude and refined products compared with September, down to 8.3m barrels. This was the lowest level since December 2024. Owners withdrew Aframax and Suezmax tankers from Russian trade routes, moving them to the Persian Gulf for more lucrative international work.

Freight economics and rising insurance costs accelerated the exit. Earnings on Baltic-to-India routes fell $46,300 a day below the global Suezmax index, while war-risk insurance premiums for Black Sea loadings peaked at $5 a barrel. In response, other tankers carrying diesel followed the Agioi Fanendes. The Talisman anchored off Alexandria in Egypt, while the Leruo changed course for Brazil and turned off its transponders.

Officially, Moscow denies any disruption to exports. Kirill Dmitriev, a special presidential envoy, dismissed reports of vessels changing course as fake news. Dmitry Peskov, the Kremlin spokesman, said all exported diesel would find buyers. Yet total seaborne crude exports fell by 9% month-on-month in October to 3.7m barrels a day. A steeper drop was prevented only by a 33% surge in northern shipments.

Shifting to crude exports does not cover the shortfall in public finances caused by lower refining. Idled refining capacity damages related tax bases, forcing auditors to look for revenue directly in extraction. In its report for January to June 2026, the Accounts Chamber noted the risk of a 47.9% drop in additional income tax (AIT) revenues, down to 1.07trn roubles.

To prevent revenue shortfalls, the watchdog launched a full audit of the AIT, which replaced the mineral extraction tax (MET) on complex fields. The first public target was RNG, which develops the Eastern Blocks of the Srednebotuobinskoye field in Yakutia, with recoverable reserves of 40.5m tonnes. Auditors demanded tax returns and payments for 2019–25, well flow rates, and initial business plans submitted to the Energy Ministry and Finance Ministry, aiming to compare promised returns with actual fiscal receipts.

RNG viewed the audit demands as pressure threatening executive disqualification and frozen accounts, successfully securing a temporary injunction from the Moscow Arbitration Court. Yet the lawsuit highlights a macroeconomic pivot. When refinery strikes strip the budget of fuel export margins, the state shifts financial pressure from foreign trade to clawing back tax breaks from domestic resource producers.

Still, the exit of European shipowners may prove temporary. Analysts at the Centre for Price Indices estimate that by late October surplus Suezmax capacity emerged in North-West Europe. In early November a similar excess of unused Aframax vessels appeared in the Mediterranean. This global oversupply could force private operators to ignore risks and return to Baltic routes.

Yet even a swift return of Greek ships cannot restore export chains without physical fuel. As long as primary crude refining remains capped at 500,000 tonnes a day owing to damaged units, international deals lack physical backing. Extra vessels cannot fix destroyed refining capacity. Continuing seaborne exports at previous levels risks deepening domestic fuel shortages.

As a result, disrupted trade contracts are forcing the state to alter its budget strategy. Lost oil product export revenues and squeezed refining margins shift the fiscal burden onto the domestic resource sector. Clawing back tax breaks and auditing excess profit taxes on major fields are direct consequences of the industry's inability to guarantee physical fuel exports.

As the Agioi Fanendes continues to manoeuvre 400 miles off New York awaiting orders from Eastmed, the deal announced by the American president has reached a dead end. The political arrangement over diesel supplies has foundered on the burnt-out columns of Russian refineries. The cost of this Atlantic geopolitical shift is now translating into tough legal battles between the Accounts Chamber and resource producers in Yakutia.
