Why European diplomats are attending talks uninvited

As delegations discuss security guarantees, both sides intensify strikes on oil refineries and power grids.

9 November 2026

Diplomatic preparations in the capital of the United Arab Emirates began with a sharp division of space. At the trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi on 10–11 November, the official table is set for just three key participants tasked with discussing mutual security guarantees behind closed doors. This is the first attempt to resume a full-fledged dialogue after a long pause. The main anomaly of the upcoming meeting lies in who has been pushed outside the negotiating room, rather than in its agenda.

The lineup illustrates the actual balance of power at the table. Russia’s delegation is led by presidential aide Vladimir Medinsky. Kyiv’s delegation is headed by the director of the Foreign Intelligence Service, Rustem Umerov. The American team is represented by special envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. National security advisers from Britain, France and Germany are arriving in the UAE at the same time. Yet no seats at the main table were allocated to diplomats from the European "troika".

European officials explicitly expect only to stay nearby, gathering first-hand information in the hotel corridors. Such a setup, where the continent’s security architecture is discussed without the direct participation of leading European states, leaves Brussels, London, Paris and Berlin as outside observers. The attempt by senior advisers to cling to the sidelines appears to be a desperate bid to preserve the appearance of influence over a process whose key parameters are being set by others.

Europe's presence in the corridors highlights a fundamental shift in international relations. Countries whose economic and social spheres bear the main risks of the conflict lack a voice when terms for ending it are drafted. The meeting in Abu Dhabi presents European elites with a stark question about the price of relying on decisions made by others. The real mechanics of diplomacy demonstrate that formal ally status guarantees no seat at the table when strategic interests are discussed.

Yet behind the diplomatic facade and peace rhetoric lies the grim calculus of military escalation. On the eve of the meeting in the UAE, settlement talk clashed with a large-scale strike on infrastructure. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky reported that Russia launched 172 drones and 36 ballistic missiles at Ukrainian territory, including North Korean-produced KN-23 missiles. Attacks on energy targets caused complete blackouts in five regions and disrupted power transmission in Kyiv—described directly as an attempt by Moscow to establish a strong negotiating position before consultations begin.

Kyiv’s retaliatory strikes showed that escalation is mutual and targets key refining assets. Alexander Drozdenko, governor of the Leningrad region, reported the destruction of 113 drones over the region. A drone hit on the KINEF plant in Kirishi caused a fire at one of Russia’s largest oil refineries. At the same time, the Saratov refinery came under attack, while a total of 411 Ukrainian drones were intercepted overnight across ten Russian regions and adjacent waters.

The synchronous strikes on critical infrastructure immediately before the delegations meet lay bare the mechanism of the upcoming bargaining. Participants seek to enter the conference room having inflicted maximum damage, converting the physical destruction of energy links into diplomatic leverage. Every struck substation and damaged refining unit becomes a bargaining chip. In this framework, intense bombardments on the eve of the meeting serve as a direct continuation of dialogue rather than an impediment to it.

Behind closed doors, diplomatic phrasing gives way to calculations by military establishments. Preparing for the UAE meeting, Vladimir Putin assembled permanent members of the Security Council on 6 November and approved a delegation that includes Igor Kostyukov, head of the GRU, and Yuri Zhigarlovsky, deputy head of the General Staff’s Main Operational Directorate. The presence of intelligence chiefs and generals alongside Vladimir Medinsky indicates that Moscow views diplomacy as a direct extension of conditions on the front line. Defence Ministry officials are tasked with setting terms in the language of maps and operational reports rather than abstract intentions.

Kyiv’s position is shored up by a financial injection intended to offset resource shortages and reinforce the negotiators' resolve. The European Commission transferred €4.36bn to Ukraine ahead of schedule, with most aimed at plugging critical budget holes. The funds allow Kyiv to avoid immediate default and give it room to manoeuvre in Abu Dhabi. Yet the American side has already set bounds on acceptable compromise: Donald Trump has publicly suggested that after fighting ends, “Ukraine will look a little different”.

While European donors cover Kyiv’s deficit, the White House is building a parallel track of global diplomacy. Donald Trump confirmed the possibility of a trilateral meeting with Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping at the APEC summit in Shenzhen on 18–19 November, where the agenda will extend far beyond Eastern Europe. Washington plans to discuss nuclear disarmament and an Iranian settlement with Moscow and Beijing, leaving Brussels’ interests aside. In this architecture, a regional conflict risks becoming a bargaining chip among three great powers.

The attempt by European officials to push into the meeting room hits an institutional dead end. Kaja Kallas, the EU’s foreign affairs chief, and Boris Pistorius, Germany’s defence minister, publicly oppose deals struck behind Europe’s back, insisting on preserving the existing security architecture grounded in NATO. Yet Brussels lacks real tools to force its presence on the talks' participants. The billions of euros sent to Kyiv serve to cover budget deficits, but do not buy a veto over key peace terms.

Europe’s exclusion is fuelling sharp friction within the bloc itself. Giorgia Meloni, Italy’s prime minister, and Radoslaw Sikorski, Poland’s foreign minister, openly criticize the admission of advisers from the "big three"—Britain, France and Germany—to the sidelines. Rome and Warsaw fear with reason that the informal presence of these three nations in the corridors will spark fresh disputes over representation within the EU. Instead of a united front, European diplomacy is sinking into internal feuds over nominal access to observe the proceedings.

The Russian position regarding European claims remains unyielding. Dmitry Peskov, press secretary to Russia's president, explicitly ruled out EU participation behind closed doors in Abu Dhabi, citing the agreed trilateral format. Moscow stresses that the European leaders' goal of inflicting defeat on Russia makes talks with them impossible. Washington pragmatically uses this refusal to strip excess voices from consultations, expediting direct bargaining with the main combatants.

In this configuration, the European region risks ending up as the principal loser. Deprived of direct access to closed sessions, European states lose the ability to protect their energy and defence priorities. The continent's entire security structure becomes a bargaining chip among external players, while European diplomats remain spectators. The emerging balance shows that being the primary financial donor no longer guarantees a seat in the great-power club.

The main caveat of the upcoming round is how limited expectations are for the talks in the Emirates. Experts describe the Abu Dhabi meeting as a straightforward attempt to “compare notes”. The diplomatic track has been frozen since February, when Washington abruptly pivoted to the war with Iran. Over the nine-month pause, both sides have merely raised the stakes in their economic and military confrontation.

For the Ukrainian side, consultations occur against a backdrop of growing physical depletion of air defence systems. Volodymyr Zelensky publicly highlighted a severe shortage of interceptor missiles for Patriot batteries, urging the United States to step up deliveries immediately. Western financial support only partially offsets this vulnerability: the European Commission’s early tranche of €4.36bn plugs budget holes, but cannot protect the power grid. Without new missiles, Kyiv is forced to negotiate from a weakened position.

Meanwhile, European officials find themselves powerless observers of a historical shift. While diplomats from the European "troika" hope to stay nearby in hotel corridors, key decisions are made without them. Brussels, Berlin and Paris pay the Ukrainian state’s current bills, but the actual course is charted by delegations inside the room. Seats at the table belong to those wielding military power; those footing the bill remain outside.

Ultimately, the trilateral format in the UAE illustrates the mechanics of modern diplomacy. Mutual security guarantees will be discussed behind closed doors, while outside, European observers await news on the fate of their own continent's security framework. The first consultations after a long pause will merely establish the parties' readiness for further bargaining. Negotiation is just beginning, but the main loser was determined before it even started.
