Kerosene export ban extended until spring

Following refinery strikes, the domestic market is being protected by imports

28 November 2026

Overnight on 28 November air-defence units shot down 1246 Ukrainian drones over Russian regions. The raid affected 16 regions, with the main thrust targeting fuel logistics facilities. A fire broke out at the Novoshakhtinsk oil products plant in the Rostov region following a second attack in a matter of days. Meanwhile, in the Samara region, drones struck the Transneft system's Samara station for the second time in a month. This hub near the village of Prosvet receives crude via four trunk pipelines from Western Siberia, Tatarstan, the Orenburg region and Kazakhstan.

A comparison of military bulletins with the state of refineries reveals a gap between official reports and economic reality. The bulletins record the destruction of aerial targets, yet isolated strikes disable key fractionation and cracking units. Damaged equipment requires complex imported components, delaying repairs for months. Every suspended refining unit immediately hits product balances.

On the day of the massive raid, the government effectively conceded that it could not balance the fuel market through conventional domestic measures. This analysis answers three questions. Why do air-defence successes fail to save the domestic market from fuel shortages? How has a major hydrocarbon exporter come to replace lost volumes with foreign purchases? And who will cover the costs of export restrictions until the spring of 2027?

Military statistics emphasise interceptions. Of the 462 drones aimed at the Moscow region, 123 were shot down on approach to the capital. Yet a high interception rate does not offset physical damage to industry. The government's decision provided clear evidence of this asymmetry: the cabinet extended the embargo on jet fuel exports from 1 December 2026 until 31 March 2027.

The export ban on kerosene was first introduced in June 2026 after an initial series of refinery strikes. The restriction was intended to last until 30 November to stabilise supplies to airlines, but unit repair schedules slipped. Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak described the market situation as 'fairly tense'. He confirmed a deficit caused by idle, damaged capacity.

To compensate for lost output, the state had to resort to purchasing fuel abroad. The kerosene shortfall is being covered by external supplies from neighbouring and distant countries. This transformation turns a major vendor of oil products into an importer, shifting logistics costs onto the domestic economy.

The kerosene situation is part of a broader refining crisis. According to Mr Novak, officials face a similar decision on petrol in December. They must decide whether to extend export restrictions beyond January and budget for foreign purchases throughout 2027. The outcome hinges on the refineries' ability to restart processing before the spring demand peak.

Defence ministry reports list the interception of 506 drones over the Bryansk region. Over the Kaluga region, air defence destroyed 120 drones. Yet isolated breakthroughs inflict targeted damage on secondary refining. To prevent domestic shortages and price spikes, regulators are deliberately sacrificing exporters' profits, forcing energy groups to forfeit foreign markets.

The halt to kerosene exports leaves a narrow corridor for state priorities. A decree signed by Mikhail Mishustin specifies that restrictions do not affect supplies to the Russian military, the Baikonur cosmodrome or infrastructure on Svalbard. The government maintained exemptions for intergovernmental agreements and humanitarian shipments, while reserving three classified clauses marked 'for official use'.

The primary vulnerability of the fuel system is the geographical concentration of transport hubs. Repeated strikes on the Samara station highlight systemic risks across the network. Four trunk oil pipelines converge on the station, making it a critical link in supplying several major refineries.

As refinery repair cycles stretch beyond normal limits, regulation has shifted into manual control mode. Industry leaders must balance export losses for oil companies against the need to import fuel until the spring of 2027. Physical constraints on repairs keep the market under constant strain.

Costs extend beyond fuel tanks. Temporary restrictions at Vnukovo and Domodedovo airports disrupt aviation. In the Penza region, authorities temporarily restricted mobile internet, while missile alert regimes were activated in the Voronezh, Lipetsk and Orenburg regions.

Disruption to logistics poses risks to the agricultural and industrial sectors. In the Bryansk region, a drone attack on a Miratorg bus in the village of Klimovo injured nine people, while another worker was injured at a facility in Starodub. In the Belgorod region, 52 drone strikes were recorded in a single day, knocking out lorries and agricultural machinery.

In seeking to insulate the domestic market from shortages, regulators face a redistribution of costs. Lost export revenues and fuel import expenses add to rising insurance premiums. Deferring unit repairs until the spring of 2027 turns temporary bans into a persistent inflationary driver.

Replacing lost output through foreign supplies increases reliance on logistical intermediaries. Until refining returns to design capacity, regulators will have to sacrifice revenue from crude exports to sustain baseline economic activity.

Supplies for the military, the Baikonur cosmodrome and facilities on Svalbard shield strategic consumers from supply shocks. Yet maintaining these isolated channels comes at the cost of eliminating commercial exports and reallocating domestic resources.

Strikes on key junction points hit the arteries feeding raw materials to a cluster of refineries. Amid the protracted recovery of the Novoshakhtinsk refinery, official claims of downed drones do not alter the core reality: the physical resilience of the fuel system has diminished.

A forced shift to foreign purchases of kerosene and petrol until March 2027 alters the country's standing in global energy markets. Rather than capturing refining margins, it must pay for long transport routes and intermediary commissions.

Physical limits on repair cycles under external pressure reveal the bounds of administrative leverage. The true picture of the fuel market is dictated by downtime schedules for secondary processing units. Until key capacities return online, the market will remain hostage to import prices and vulnerable transport hubs.
