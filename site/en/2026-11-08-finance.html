A sharp reduction in subsidies forces banks to return borrowers to the market

Family mortgage originations fell by 77% in October, leaving market rates of 19% as the only option for most households.

8 November 2026

In October 2026 the Russian mortgage market recorded its sharpest monthly contraction in nine months. According to data from Frank RG, an analytical firm, commercial banks issued 308.8bn roubles in home loans—nearly half the total volume of the previous month. By number of loans, the market shrank by 40%, to just 75,500. This was the lowest figure since February 2026, breaking the previous upward trend.

Spokesmen for the largest banks describe the October figures as a technical correction following a surge in September, when mortgage lending reached a year-to-date record of 608.5bn roubles. That earlier spike reflected borrowers rushing to complete transactions before terms tightened, rather than new long-term demand. Once this front-loaded demand dried up, lending volumes collapsed.

The slump in the subsidized sector exposes a core vulnerability in the current development model: without state subsidies, retail demand cannot sustain previous sales volumes. Attempts to push clients toward standard commercial mortgages run into household financial constraints. Government cuts to support schemes are forcing banks to revise risk assessments and alter how they deal with retail clients.

The scale of the contraction highlights the retail lending sector's reliance on state subsidies. This analysis examines how the curtailment of preferential programmes alters lending patterns, why the shift to commercial loans creates risks for the financial system, and who will ultimately pay for the market's transformation.

Beneath talk of technical adaptation lies a sharp contraction in state-backed lending. Analysts estimate that subsidized mortgage originations fell by roughly two-thirds in October, to 145bn roubles. The drop was steepest in the family mortgage scheme, where lending fell by 77% to 92bn roubles. Unsubsidized commercial lending declined much less, down 9% to 163bn roubles.

At leading lenders, the divergence is starker. At Sberbank, family mortgage originations fell by a factor of 4.4 compared with September, barely exceeding 69bn roubles. At VTB, the share of subsidized loans in total lending fell from 52% to 38%; at Dom.RF Bank, it dropped from 73% to 61%. T-Bank suspended subsidized originations entirely, redirecting clients to commercial products.

Demand cooled immediately after the Finance Ministry tightened subsidy rules on 1 October. The maximum subsidy period was capped at 15 years, and interest rates for families with one or two children were raised to 8–12%. The previous rate of 6% was retained only for borrowers providing a down payment of at least 50%. Because families with one or two children previously accounted for 90% of originations, higher demand from larger families failed to fill the gap.

As the subsidized segment shrank, the share of commercial mortgages rose from 29% to 53% in October. This shift reflects administrative cuts to state programmes rather than organic growth. With weighted-average market rates at 18–19%, according to Dom.RF, commercial originations cannot offset the loss of subsidized lending.

To soften the fall in lending, banks turned to teaser-rate schemes. These offer reduced payments for the first one or two years at a nominal rate of 5%, before jumping to market rates of 18%. The Bank of Russia views such structures as a systemic risk and has drafted regulatory hurdles. It plans to issue draft rules in the first quarter of next year, to take effect from 1 July 2027. Under the new rules, banks must set aside an additional 50 percentage points in reserves for loans where monthly payments rise by more than 20% during the first three years compared with the preceding 12 months.

Regulatory action against high-risk schemes leaves developers and lenders facing high interest rates. Expectations of a quick reduction in borrowing costs clash with medium-term forecasts. The Bank of Russia expects its key rate to average 10.5–12.5% in 2027, with a return to the target range of 7.5–8.5% not anticipated before 2029. Commercial mortgage rates will therefore remain at 18–19% for an extended period, depressing demand.

Government measures to cushion the cuts are narrowly targeted and alter little overall. The Finance Ministry's expansion of an 8% subsidized mortgage scheme (requiring a 20% down payment) to Crimea and Sevastopol covers a region of over 900,000 households. Yet such local initiatives offer only a cosmetic impression of continued support as most buyers are funneled into the commercial market. As banks take two to six months to adapt, housing affordability will continue to decline for most families.

The restructuring of state subsidies extends beyond residential property. In industry, as in housing, the government is tightening access to state support. Prime Minister Mikhail Mishustin signed a decree altering property management rules in Advanced Special Economic Zones (ASEZs). Investors stripped of resident status for failing to meet capital investment or employment commitments may now buy out their leased land.

The main condition for buyout is active production: completed capital structures must cover at least 10% of the land area. Terms for non-compliant firms differ sharply from subsidized rates. While active residents buy land at cadastral value, former participants must pay full market price. The Far East Development Corporation noted that the mechanism allows operating businesses to retain assets and secure bank loans using them as collateral.

At the same time, costs are rising for active members of preferential zones. The buyout price for real estate from ASEZ management companies will rise from 2.5% to 30% of cadastral value. Additional proceeds from buyouts and commercial rents from non-residents are earmarked for infrastructure development. Management companies may now hire third-party operators rather than maintain facilities themselves.

The Finance Ministry and cabinet are pursuing a clear objective: replacing direct state aid with commercial self-sufficiency. In both mortgage lending and industrial zones, the budget will no longer cover unfulfilled commitments or hidden subsidies. Businesses and households must pay full market rates to retain assets or maintain operations.

Shifting the financial burden from the federal budget to borrowers and developers alters the financial sector's operating model. Analysts estimate banks will need two to six months to adapt to the new rules for family mortgages. Yet hopes of near-term relief conflict with the Bank of Russia's mid-term policy stance: a return to a 7.5–8.5% key rate is projected only for 2029. High borrowing costs are becoming permanent, making commercial lending at 18–19% unviable as a temporary bridge.

A similar selectivity applies to businesses in preferential zones. Raising the property buyout price from 2.5% to 30% of cadastral value aims to eliminate quasi-subsidies and redirect funds to infrastructure. For former ASEZ residents, purchasing land under existing structures at market rates is the sole path to preserving assets for bank collateral. The state supports only active enterprises, leaving others to pay for infrastructure themselves.

Simultaneously, the central bank is curtailing informal mechanisms used to stimulate demand. The planned 50-percentage-point reserve requirement increase on mortgages where payments rise over 20% after three years makes low-down-payment schemes unviable for banks. Regulatory restrictions eliminate high-risk practices, depriving developers of subsidized workarounds and buyers of artificial grace periods.

The result is an economic landscape in which targeted state programmes cannot offset a broader liquidity deficit. The housing market and industrial sector face strict capital rationing, with all participants paying full market rates for finance and public infrastructure. For households and businesses, cheap state support has ended, replaced by an era of persistently high interest rates.
