State reinsurer may strip cover from civil aircraft

Claims at the company exceeded 90bn roubles in nine months. Risks for 600 leased aircraft could be shifted to airlines

27 November 2026

Russian National Reinsurance Company (RNRC) has been holding confidential consultations with major air carriers since early October 2026 regarding a fundamental revision of current contract terms. The state monopoly reinsurer sent market participants formal notices of its intention from 1 January 2027 to withdraw fully from covering war risks and sabotage, or to cut liability limits for these events sharply. For the country's transport system, this means dismantling the key security loop created as an emergency measure four years ago.

The standard insurance scheme for civil commercial flights comes under direct threat. Without full state support, the commercial insurance market lacks sufficient capital to cover the aviation industry's catastrophic risks independently. Without RNRC's involvement, primary insurers will be unable to issue policies with adequate cover, creating risks of operational paralysis.

A strict legal link between aircraft and active lease agreements sharpens the problem. The absence of war-risk reinsurance gives lessors legal grounds to demand the immediate return of jets or full compensation for lost cover. Airlines face a grim choice: take massive risks onto their own balance-sheets or ground mainline aircraft.

This analysis addresses two key questions surrounding the transformation. It explains why a state entity is forced to trim insurance limits for air carriers right now. It also shows how market participants will attempt to operate once the familiar state umbrella effectively ceases to exist.

For four years, operators in aviation and industrial real estate acted on the assumption that state backing was indefinite and financially bottomless. The reinsurer’s financial performance this year marked a turning point. In the first nine months of 2026, RNRC's payouts nearly doubled compared with previous periods, exceeding 90bn roubles. A wave of strikes on industrial facilities and civil infrastructure drove this surge in losses. The state reserve mechanism simply could not sustain the physical scale of the damage.

The retrenchment of cover did not begin in transport. Business received its first warning in the summer of 2026, when RNRC applied stricter terms to commercial and industrial real-estate owners exposed to new threats. By early October, the model for reinsuring war risks and sabotage was clearly broken. Total exposure surpassed the company's capitalisation, while the prospect of unlimited fiscal bailouts ran into the hard constraints of the state treasury.

Cold accounting logic took over. Long-standing clients paying regular premiums without generating losses could no longer expect automatic renewals. Annual premium inflows from airlines paled beside potential payouts for destroyed mainline aircraft in wartime incidents. RNRC chose to avert its own default by curtailing limits, prioritising the financial stability of the state institution by shifting risk onto the sector.

The scale of the problem lays bare the core conflict: the state entity is bound by the arithmetic of its own balance-sheet. With total payouts exceeding 90bn roubles, maintaining open-ended war-risk guarantees created a dangerous breach in RNRC's capital. The state reinsurer decided to exit aviation risks ahead of 1 January 2027, confronting the market with a reality: the old model of sovereign-backed guarantees is exhausted.

Russian civil aviation’s financial structure relies on a three-tier chain of risk distribution. Airlines pay regular premiums to commercial insurers, which retain only standard operational risks. Insurers pass the most onerous and unpredictable portion—war damage, terrorism and sabotage—entirely to the state monopoly. Without RNRC’s reinsurance capacity, no private insurer in the country has the capital to compensate for lost aircraft, leaving smaller carriers most vulnerable to rule changes. Consequently, any catastrophic hull write-offs fall back on primary insurers unable to absorb the blow.

This setup is tied to the legal framework of the domestic fleet. Russia currently operates over 600 mainline aircraft, the vast majority of which are leased. Standard contracts require operators to maintain maximum insurance cover at all times, including war risk. A withdrawal of reinsurance strips carriers of protection and gives lessors legal grounds to demand the immediate return of jets or full compensation for lost aircraft. For most airlines, such claims mean a direct route to grounding operations.

The situation echoes the events of spring 2022 almost verbatim. Four years ago, foreign reinsurers stopped covering war risks for Russian carriers, and RNRC stepped in to absorb those liabilities, saving the fleet from repossession. Now the state institution is reversing the mechanism, pushing risks back onto airlines. Market participants will have to find vast sums from revenue to cover potential losses or accept the grounding of mainline jets. Without the sovereign umbrella, the entire structure loses its guaranteed payer of last resort.

Aviation industry figures are seeking to soften the crisis, stressing the systemic importance of domestic and international flights. Sources close to the Transport Ministry remain confident that none of the more than 600 mainline aircraft will be grounded solely over insurance disputes. Meanwhile, Andrey Borisov, president of the Air Transport Operators Association, appealed to RNRC not to make final decisions without detailed prior discussions with airlines and insurers. The market still hopes to shift negotiations from a total withdrawal of cover to a revision of premium rates.

The insurance sector points out a flaw in the state monopoly’s logic. Ilya Kabachnik, deputy chief executive of AlfaStrakhovanie, emphasises that repricing cover is fundamentally different from denying protection to clients who pay millions in premiums annually without generating losses. Yet for RNRC, client loyalty yields to immediate financial realities: the firm's payouts in nine months of 2026 exceeded 90bn roubles. With private insurers unable to take on war risks without state backing, arguments about clean claims histories lose their force.

Sovereign priorities are rewriting the rules of financial exchange. When potential payouts threaten the state reinsurer’s stability, the system sacrifices sector-specific cover to preserve institutional capital. Open-ended state backing has hit a hard limit. Fiscal resources no longer serve as an infinite guarantee for aviation risks; shifting liability back to airlines marks a fundamental shift in the model of state obligations.

Both RNRC and trade associations stress that no final decision has been formally made. RNRC confirms only preliminary discussions, noting that no single position has been reached on revising active contracts. Mr Borisov has asked the reinsurer’s leadership not to issue final rulings without consulting carriers and primary insurers. This pause offers the market hope of a compromise through higher premiums, yet the trajectory towards withdrawing state funds from war risk cover remains unchanged.

Talks have been under way since early October, but the deadline for the new terms is fixed for early January 2027. Airlines have little time to adapt financial models or negotiate softer policy terms with lessors. Without state backing, carriers must cover losses from sabotage or military incidents out of retained earnings. For smaller operators, a single claim under such a financial burden could prove fatal, pushing the industry towards fleet reductions and consolidation.

The shift exposes the vulnerability of the framework built under external pressure. The reinsurance mechanism relied on the assumption that the state insurance system could absorb losses indefinitely. Yet reserve limits and increasing attacks on infrastructure forced regulators to choose between protecting a single industry and preserving institutional balance-sheet stability. Civil aviation’s interests surrendered to the need to safeguard the sovereign reinsurer’s capital.

The story comes full circle. In 2022 RNRC emerged as the sector’s saviour, assuming war risks after foreign reinsurers exited and averting the mass seizure of mainline jets. Four years later, the architect is dismantling the national protection system it built. The temporary lifeline has served its purpose, giving way to a harsher reality where airlines must pay for their own risks.
